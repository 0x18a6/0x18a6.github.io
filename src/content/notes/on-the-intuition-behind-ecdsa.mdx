I'm reading this blog post [here](https://rareskills.io/post/ecdsa-tutorial), as well as the prerequesites, to rediscover ECDSA from first principles.

Why do we need anything more than $P = p.G$?

We're building a digital signature algorithm. $P = p.G$ already proves knowledge of a secret $p$ that corresponds to $P$.

$p$ is for signing, $P$ is for verifying. $P$ is a commitment to the value of $p$. 

Ok, $p$ is for signing, but signing what? We need to introduce $m$, the message to sign.

Something like:
$sig = p.m$, where $P = p.G$

How to verify?

This is the beauty of ECC. We raise the scalars into curve points by multiplying with the generator: $G.sig = G.p.m$. With $P = p.G$, we get: $G.sig = P.m$, which is all public information.

Because both $sig$ and $m$ are public, $p$ is leaked 💀. This begs hiding $p$.

We introduce a second secret $q$, where $Q = q.G$. Now we have two secrets. It's not possible to solve for $p$ with just the single $sig$ equation. An attacker will need a second equation.

This prevents direct algebraic recovery from that single equation.       

I'm writing $sig = f(p,m,q)$ because I'm not sure how to apply $q$ there yet.

Back to the beauty of ECC, raise scalars into curve points ✨.

Because we have two secrets on the right side of $sig=f(p,m,q)$, we will have only one $G$ on that side for two secrets, the other secret will not be raised to its public commitment.

But we have $G$ on the left side too, that's why $f$ needs to raise $q^{-1}$, we get: $G.q.sig = G.p.m$.

That's equivalent to $Q.sig = P.m$, all public information that the verifier verifies. The signature is $sig.q^{-1} = p.m$
